Why You've Stopped Improving at Padel (and What Structured Training Changes)

By The Padel Camp team · October 26, 2026

The first year of padel is fast. Every month you win more points, the walls make more sense, and the game feels easier. Then, somewhere along the way, progress slows down. You play three times a week, but your level stays the same. The same opponents beat you in the same way.

This is one of the most common stages in padel, and it has clear reasons.

1. Matches repeat what you already do

In a match you use the shots you trust and avoid the ones you don't. That is the right way to win a point, and the wrong way to improve. After a hundred matches, your strong shots are a bit stronger and your weak ones are exactly where they were.

2. Nobody tells you what is going wrong

It is hard to see your own game. You feel that a bandeja "didn't work", but not whether the problem was your feet, your contact point or your choice of shot. Partners give advice, but often it is a guess, and sometimes it contradicts what the last partner said.

3. Habits harden

Every time you repeat a movement, it becomes more automatic, including the inefficient ones. A grip that is slightly wrong, a backswing that is too big, standing too far from the net: after a year of matches these feel normal, and changing them feels uncomfortable at first.

4. Tactics stay instinctive

Many players at the intermediate level lose points not because of technique but because of decisions: going for a winner from the back of the court, leaving the net too early, lobbing at the wrong moment. Without someone pointing these patterns out, they keep happening.

What breaks a plateau

Playing more rarely helps on its own. What helps is a short period of focused work:

  • An honest assessment of where your game is, so you work on what matters, not on what you happen to enjoy.
  • Repetition with feedback. Many balls on the same shot, with a coach correcting you while the movement is fresh.
  • Small groups. With four players on a court, you get far more touches and more personal attention than in a class of eight.
  • Tactics practised, not just explained. Real match situations repeated until the right choice becomes natural.
  • A clear plan afterwards. Knowing what to practise in your regular games, so the progress continues after the training ends.

This is the logic behind a structured camp. A few days of concentrated work won't change everything, but they give you a precise picture of your game and the first steps out of the plateau.
